Where Cape Town can still build

A land-screening model for low-income housing across the City of Cape Town metro. Thirty candidate precincts scored on fourteen criteria — job reach, transit, servicing, flood and heat exposure, environmental constraint, delivery risk, and the Group Areas geography the city still runs on — then ranked three ways: on site quality, on how much housing that quality actually delivers, and on whether building there undoes or repeats the removals.

Provenance

Read this before the numbers. Six of the fourteen criteria are now measured, not guessed: transit distance from 131 real OSM rail stations, social-facility access from 1 642 mapped schools, clinics and hospitals, terrain from SRTM 30 m, plus job accessibility and land supply computed from coordinates. Eight remain desktop analyst estimates — flood, contamination, environmental sensitivity, bulk infrastructure, delivery complexity, heat, and the two justice indices. This is a screening layer for deciding where to spend survey budget, not a feasibility study. Basemap geometry is real OpenStreetMap data. Group Areas Act designations are historical record; the redress and displacement scores built on them are analyst judgements, and are meant to be argued with. Layer 05 shows how far the ranking moves when those remaining estimates are wrong.

Layer 03 · Spatial justice

The land question the score keeps quiet

A weighted sum over land economics is not neutral in a city zoned by race. It rewards cheap, peripheral land — which is exactly the land apartheid planning assigned to black and coloured residents. So the model runs twice.

Under the Group Areas Act of 1950, Cape Town was carved into racially designated zones. Roughly 60 000 people, overwhelmingly classified coloured, were removed from District Six after it was declared white in 1966. Simon's Town's Luyolo and its coloured community were cleared. Ndabeni, the African location created by the 1901 plague removals, was cleared for industry. Khayelitsha was founded in 1983 to move African residents to the far edge of the Cape Flats; Atlantis was built in 1976 as a coloured "growth point" forty kilometres from the city. Mitchells Plain was built in the 1970s to house the removed.

That geography did not dissolve in 1994. It is the metro's current land-price surface, its commute times, and its servicing map — which means a model that optimises on land economics will faithfully reproduce it while appearing to be merely technical.

The redress scenario adds two criteria the efficiency scenario has no place for:

  • Spatial redress (weight 0.17) — high where a site puts low-income households back inside well-located, job-rich land that the Act reserved for white occupation. Low where a site simply adds density to the townships apartheid built.
  • Displacement risk (weight 0.06, a cost) — its necessary counterweight. Woodstock, Salt River and Observatory score well on redress and badly here: the same inner-city location that makes them valuable is what is currently evicting the working-class, largely coloured community already living there. Redress that displaces is not redress.

Neither scenario is the right answer. The movement between them is the finding.

Layer 04 · Zoning & height

Half the land is already illegal to build on

Every yield figure above assumes the land can be built to the density its typology implies. Current zoning says otherwise for most of it — and the block falls hardest on exactly the land that would deliver redress.

These ten sites carry a zone that permits no residential use whatsoever. Eight of them are already publicly owned.

The state owns the problem and wrote the rule

455 hectares and 62,930 units — half the entire programme — sit on land that government already owns and its own zoning forbids housing on. Wingfield and Ysterplaat are military reserve (TR2). Culemborg is rail reserve. Ndabeni, Epping and Athlone are industrial. Woodstock Hospital and Helen Bowden are community zoning. Two Rivers is open space. None of these needs a willing seller. All of them need a rezoning the same government controls.

The block tracks redress

Of the units on high-redress land, 80% require rezoning. On low-redress land the figure is 32%. The ten zoning-blocked sites average a redress score of 76 against 57 for everything else.

This is not a coincidence and it is not a correlation worth quoting site by site — per site the relationship is weak. It is an artefact of what kind of land ended up well located: military bases, rail yards and industrial estates were placed inside the historically white core and zoned for anything but housing. The apartheid city protected itself twice, first by Group Areas and then by land-use.

What the tranching quietly did

Tranche 1 is 98% buildable under current zoning — 7,750 of its 7,912 units. That reads like good news and is not. The readiness score that built the tranches selected for sites that are already correctly zoned, which in this metro means the small inner-city parcels. The near-term programme is deliverable precisely because it avoids everything that matters.

What follows from this

  • The binding constraint on Cape Town’s housing pipeline is not land and not money. It is a rezoning queue. Spend the first two years on land-use applications for Wingfield, Ysterplaat and Culemborg, not on the 528 units at Founders Garden.
  • A blanket public-land rezoning instrument — one process covering all eight publicly owned blocked sites — is worth more than any individual project approval.
  • Two Rivers and Philippi East are different in kind: their zones (open space, agricultural) reflect live environmental and food-security objections, not administrative inertia. Do not bundle them with the military and rail land.

Zone codes and the storey limits attached to them are the City’s Development Management Scheme. The assignment of a zone to each site is analyst-coded from its current use and applicable overlay, not read off the City’s cadastral zoning layer. Replace it from the Open Data Portal before acting on any of this.

Layer 06 · Findings

What the screen says

The estimates are a bigger risk than the weights

Re-weighting the model ±30% leaves nine sites reliably in the top ten. Jittering the remaining estimated inputs by only ±15% leaves seven, and moves the average site 4.9 places. Founders Garden, Bellville CBD and District Six are immovable; Prestwich Precinct holds a top-ten place in barely a third of draws. Read the top of the table as a shortlist, not an order of merit — and note that the fix is survey work, not more modelling.

Zoning, not land, is the binding constraint

Only 35% of the screened yield can be built under current zoning. The other 82,200 units need a rezoning first, and 541 hectares carry a zone permitting no housing whatsoever. Everything below is subject to that: the rankings describe which land is worth unlocking, not which land is available next year. See Layer 04.

The two big parcels carry the programme

Wingfield (180 ha) and Ysterplaat (95 ha) are national-government land sitting inside the metro's servicing envelope. Between them they hold roughly 30% of all screened yield. Neither cracks the top ten on suitability — they are further from a station and carry contamination risk — but on the impact index they rank second and fourth. No other pair of sites changes the metro's housing arithmetic the way these two do.

Inner-city infill wins on quality and loses on scale

Founders Garden, Pickwick Road, Sir Lowry's Road and the Woodstock Hospital site score highest because they are minutes from work and on top of existing bulk infrastructure. Their combined yield is under 2 000 units. They are worth doing precisely because they are fast and symbolically central — not because they solve the backlog.

Redress and displacement pull in opposite directions

Woodstock Hospital, Salt River Market, Pickwick Road and Sir Lowry’s Road all fall three to four places once displacement risk is priced in. They sit on land declared white and cleared of its coloured residents — the redress case is strong — but they are also the front line of present-day eviction. Building there is defensible only with tenure protection for sitting tenants written into the disposal condition, not bolted on afterwards.

The sites that climb are the ones with redress value and nobody to displace: Dido Valley (up 7), Two Rivers (up 5), Wingfield, Culemborg and Helen Bowden (up 4). State and rail land inside the historically white core is the cleanest form this argument takes.

The Cape Flats sites fail on access, not on land

Delft, Philippi East, Macassar and Sir Lowry's Pass have land in abundance and rank in the bottom third. The model is reproducing apartheid geography as a cost: cheap land, far from work, thin servicing, high flood exposure. Building there without fixing the transit link repeats the RDP-era mistake at higher volume. Note what this means: the efficiency scenario is not neutral. Left alone it recommends the periphery because apartheid made the periphery cheap.

Where the model is least trustworthy

  • District Six ranks third on merit and lands in Tranche 3 on readiness. Restitution claims are a legal process, not a delivery-complexity score — treat its date as unknown.
  • Two Rivers Urban Park is scored down hard for environmental sensitivity and heritage. That single input is doing most of the work; a different reading of the Liesbeek/Black River floodplain would move it ten places.
  • Culemborg depends entirely on rail-yard rationalisation. If Transnet does not release, its 7 980 units are zero, not delayed.
  • Contamination at Wingfield, Ysterplaat and the Athlone Power Station is estimated, not sampled. A Phase 2 assessment could move any of them a full tranche.

What to do with this

  • Commission geotechnical and contamination survey on the top four impact sites only — that is where survey budget buys the most certainty per rand.
  • Four criteria have already been measured rather than estimated — transit distance, facility access, elevation and slope. Doing so moved 18 of 30 sites and pushed Wingfield into the top ten. Run the Earth Engine script to do the same for NDVI, NDBI, LST and impervious cover, and expect comparable movement.
  • Re-weight for a transit-led scenario (transit 0.28, job access 0.22) and see which Cape Flats sites move — that difference is the value of a corridor investment.

Layer 07 · Method

How it was built

The model

Multi-criteria decision analysis. Every criterion is min-max normalised to 0–100 across the thirty sites, with cost criteria inverted, then combined as a weighted sum. Weights are shown live in the panel opposite and sum to 1.0.

Job accessibility is the one criterion computed rather than assigned: a gravity index Σ Jₕ / (dₕ + 1)^1.6 over eight weighted metro employment nodes, with d the great-circle distance from the site. Land value is log-compressed so the Atlantic Seaboard does not flatten the axis.

Yield is developable hectares × a density assumption per typology (60 u/ha constrained greenfield up to 260 u/ha adaptive reuse), with 65% of units targeted at the sub-R3 500/month band.

Impact = suitability × log₁₀(units), renormalised. It exists because a weighted sum systematically over-rewards small, perfect parcels.

Tranche is a readiness composite of delivery complexity, bulk-infrastructure headroom and contamination burden — not a commitment date.

Two scenarios. The same fourteen criteria are weighted twice. Efficiency zeroes the two justice criteria and reads the problem as land economics. Redress gives spatial redress 0.17 and displacement risk 0.06, funded by cutting land cost and delivery complexity. Which weighting is correct is a political choice, not a technical one, so the model reports both and the movement between them.

The 3D and 4D layers

The massing study is a schematic isometric. A block is one apartment building — a single stacked residential structure with fourteen dwellings per floor plate on a double-loaded corridor, which is the ordinary delivery unit for South African social housing. Below about five storeys that is a walk-up; above it the block needs lifts, and the cost model changes with it. Storeys come from the typology's density assumption (density ÷ 19, clamped to 3–16); block count is then total yield divided by storeys × fourteen. It is a volume sanity-check, not an architectural proposal — use it to ask whether the assumed density is plausible on that footprint, and whether the resulting number of buildings is a programme anyone could actually procure.

The 4D layer runs the tranche programme year by year, with each site's units released linearly across its construction window. It answers one question: when does the pipeline actually produce housing, rather than approvals.

Weights

What is measured, and what is still a guess

Measured: transit distance (nearest of 131 OSM rail stations and halts), social-facility access (a weighted count of 1 642 mapped schools, clinics, hospitals, doctors and pharmacies within 1 km and 2 km), elevation and slope (SRTM 30 m, slope from a 300 m cross around each site). Derived: job accessibility, distances, yield, tranche, both rankings. Still estimated: flood risk, environmental sensitivity, contamination, bulk infrastructure, delivery complexity, heat anomaly, spatial redress, displacement risk — eight of fourteen.

Measuring the first four changed things. Rank correlation between estimate and measurement ran from +0.61 (facilities) to +0.79 (transit): directionally right, individually unreliable. The correction that mattered most was systematic — the estimates under-rated social facilities across the Cape Flats. Khayelitsha measures 96 out of 100 on facility access against a guess of 54; Mitchells Plain 87 against 58. The townships are not short of schools and clinics. What they lack is work and a way to reach it, which is exactly what the job and transit criteria already say.

Replacing the remaining estimates

  • Remote sensing — scripts/gee_remote_sensing.js computes NDVI, NDBI, NDWI, BSI and an impervious fraction from Sentinel-2 SR summer composites; land-surface temperature anomaly from Landsat 9 ST_B10; slope and elevation from SRTM; and built-up change 2000–2020 from JRC GHSL, all as 500 m zonal means per site.
  • Land parcels, ownership, zoning — City of Cape Town Open Data Portal cadastral and zoning layers; national land from the DPWI immovable asset register.
  • Flood — the City's 1:100-year floodline layer and the Cape Flats Aquifer extent, in place of the elevation + NDWI proxy used here.
  • Transit — now measured to the nearest OSM rail station, but still straight-line. PRASA and MyCiTi GTFS would give a real network distance and, better, a travel time.
  • Terrain — the City's 0.5 m LiDAR DSM/DTM where a real massing model is needed; SRTM is only adequate at screening scale.
  • Demand — the housing needs register and StatsSA Census small-area data, to weight sites by the backlog they serve rather than by supply alone.

Basemap geometry is a hand-simplified schematic coastline, relief and rail network in Web Mercator — correct enough to place sites in context, not a survey product. Site markers sit at true coordinates.

Layer 08 · Take it further

Where the real basemaps live

This page runs in a sandbox that blocks every external host, so it can inline real OSM geometry but never stream satellite tiles. The map above pans, zooms to 14×, goes full-screen and toggles its layers — but for imagery, four companion outputs do what a sandboxed page cannot.

Google Earth Pro — true 3D and a time slider

outputs/cape_town_housing.kml (or the .kmz). Drag it onto Google Earth Pro. Each precinct becomes an extruded footprint sized by developable area and raised to storeys × 3.2 m, coloured by tranche, over real satellite imagery and terrain. Every block carries a TimeSpan, so Earth’s own time slider runs the 2027–2037 programme. A second folder extrudes the same footprints by spatial redress score, which turns the Group Areas geography into topography you can fly through.

The same KMZ imports into Google My Maps if you want a shareable web link without a developer key.

Satellite — no API key needed

docs/satellite_map.html. The quickest route to real imagery: Leaflet with seven basemaps — Esri World Imagery with and without labels, OSM streets, light, dark, OpenTopoMap terrain and a hillshade — plus every vector layer from this analysis as toggleable overlays. Colour the precincts by tranche, suitability, impact, redress, displacement or heat anomaly, and size them by yield, hectares or social units. Serve the repo root and open /docs/satellite_map.html. No key, no account.

Google Maps — Street View and 45° tilt

docs/google_maps.html. Needs your own Maps JavaScript API key and a local server; the page walks you through both. Worth the setup for one thing the free tiles cannot do: drop into Street View on any precinct and see what is actually standing there today.

QGIS — the analyst’s copy

scripts/build_qgis_project.py builds a project with Esri World Imagery, OSM and a hillshade as XYZ layers, the real boundary/coastline/rail vectors, 1 km site catchments buffered in EPSG:32734 so the radius is honestly metric, and graduated renderers on both suitability and redress with marker size driven by dwelling yield.

Run it from the QGIS Python console:

exec(open('scripts/build_qgis_project.py').read())

This script has not been executed — QGIS was not installed on the machine that produced this analysis. It uses stable PyQGIS classes, but treat the first run as a test. outputs/candidate_sites.geojson and data/geo/basemap.geojson load into QGIS, ArcGIS or kepler.gl directly, with no script at all.
